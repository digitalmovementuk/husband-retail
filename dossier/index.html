What we do — and what we do not

We plan, lease, position and asset-manage luxury and premium retail destinations, working for the developer, the landlord or the investor who owns them — and, separately and one at a time, for a retail brand entering a new market. Our three core competencies, in order: consulting, planning, leasing.

We are not a retailing consultancy. We do not advise brands on how to sell product, run stores or build assortments. That distinction matters in every conversation, because the words "retail consultant" send people to the wrong idea of us. We are in retail real estate.

We sit, as the firm has always put it, "on the peripheries of a project… an aerial view that encompasses the needs of the developers, retailers, customers and the shape of the industry."

Management consultancies are a channel, not a rival. We are happy to be the retail real estate expert alongside them — we have worked with McKinsey more than once on exactly that basis, including at Lusail Boulevard in Doha.

What we sell

The four practice areas

Four disciplines, one partnership — from the first feasibility study to a fully leased, market-leading destination and the assets that sustain it.

  1. Development Consultancy — market analysis, retail concept development, design development and layout analysis, retail-mix planning.
  2. Leasing Strategy & Execution — leasing strategy, project marketing, negotiation, lease documentation, brand curation.
  3. Positioning & Repositioning — centre positioning, leasing and consumer marketing plans, brand development, marketing-programme management.
  4. Investment Advisory & Asset Management — acquisition and disposition advice, strategic partnership and corporate-finance solutions, operational strategy, business planning, asset plan and financial analysis.

One rule we do not bend: we only handle leasing for developments we have worked on. It is the reason landlords trust the leasing advice — we are not shopping a tenant list around.

Brands — market entry, one at a time

We take one retail brand as a client at any one time, and the service is market entry: which city, what kind of location — mall or standalone — then the research, the landlord conversations, identifying the unit and negotiating it. Once the binding lease is signed we hand the relationship over to the brand and the landlord.

It is a real estate service. We do not offer brands anything that is not real estate.

Who we are up against — and when a client needs us

Our competitors are the international agencies — Savills, JLL, Knight Frank, CBRE — and the country-specific independents, who are particularly well developed in China.

The moment a client needs us is specific. Developers have capable people in-house who handle planning and leasing perfectly well in the ordinary run of things. What they cannot absorb on top of the day job is a new or repositioned centre with hundreds of stores to lease against a fixed opening date. That is when the phone rings, and it is the moment every conversation, page and campaign should be built around.

What we bring that an agency does not: we have planned the format, not just leased the space. We know which tenant belongs on which floor and why, because we designed the mix before the building existed.

How we sound

Premium, understated, confident. British spelling. Formal but warm. Quietly authoritative — never salesy, and never shouting.

Words that are ours: iconic · luxury · coveted · destination · curated · pioneering · end-to-end · market-leading.

Words to handle with care: "consultancy" on its own reads as management consulting or retailing advice. The category is retail real estate. "Luxury" describes the destination — it does not stand alone as a description of us.

The one writing rule that matters: be concrete. "We lifted footfall at X by Y per cent" beats "we improve performance" every time. Names and numbers are the whole game — a project name, a client name, a floor, a format, a year. Where you have one, use it.

AI for retail destinations — a capability the firm can already sell

This is not theory. In June 2026 Husband Retail delivered a board-grade report, Global Retail Report: AI, to a major London estate owner. It contains a structured playbook and a staged roadmap, and it is the clearest evidence that the firm is current on the one subject most retail-centre owners are behind on.

The thesis in one line: retail centres are laggards in AI, in Asia historically and largely worldwide, while luxury brands and fast fashion — Zara, Uniqlo — are right on top of it. That gap is the opportunity. For an owner or operator, AI is a huge positive shock.

The four things AI actually does for a destination

  1. Guest-facing — search, concierge, orchestration of the digital journey.
  2. Footfall intelligence — heatmaps, route and dwell analytics, layout optimisation.
  3. Tenant analytics — leasing support and asset-performance prediction.
  4. Media monetisation — audience activation and augmented reality.

The market, in numbers

Global AI in retail is roughly USD 16bn in 2026, rising above USD 40bn by 2030 — 23% compound growth. North America holds about 40% of it; Asia-Pacific is the fastest-growing region, led by China, South Korea and India. The leading applications are personalisation (71%), marketing (49%) and chatbots (31%). By 2032, 71% of consumers expect AI to be part of their physical shopping journey.

The ideas underneath the work

Four ideas run through everything the firm does. They are worth knowing because they are what separates Husband Retail from a leasing agency, and because they should show up in how the website reads.

Destination brand, not utility shopping centre. This is the foundational distinction. A retail centre can be planned, before it opens, to be a brand and a destination — rather than a functional place to buy things. Pacific Place was the first large-scale centre in Asia conceived that way, and Paul was there for it. Everything else follows from this one idea.

Experiential 2.0. Fuse cultural institutions with retail so a destination becomes more than shopping: cultural immersion that brings cultural attractions, historical depth and shopping into a single visit. Visitors do not choose culture or shopping — they get both. That is what creates purpose and belonging, and it is what pure-experience malls cannot deliver without culture, arts and history at the core.

The customer journey is a design discipline. The clearest example is New York's High Line — a two-to-three-storey elevated wooden, landscaped, open-air walkway running a couple of kilometres through the Meatpacking District, with seating, screens and pop-ups. Paul calls it the world's first public, purposefully created customer-journey experience, built to elevate the most atmospheric tourist district in New York.

Arts, culture and AI as one layer. Not three separate initiatives — one experiential layer. Culture supplies the meaning, AI supplies the intelligence beneath it, and the retail engine turns both into a working, leased destination.

One more market read worth carrying: Asia is now the premier global retail-centre market, with far more centres than Europe, and it is the blueprint. Husband Retail was once a door opener to Asia. It now knows Asia's best practice and can export it to other markets — which is a different, and newer, axis of demand.

Hong Kong search — what people type, and what we build for it

This tab is the whole search picture in one place. It consolidates ten research documents produced between 4 and 26 August 2026: 600 competitor pages stripped for vocabulary, 531 terms judged, 50 English terms measured in Hong Kong, 72 Chinese terms hand-checked, 109 Hong Kong results pages read one at a time in a single day, and a full reading of where husband-retail.com stands in search today.

Where two of those documents disagree, the later reading wins and the disagreement is written down rather than smoothed over. Nothing here is estimated. Where a number could not be sourced, the cell says so.

The rule the whole list obeys. No page goes into production on a term that has not been measured and had its live results page read. A phrase that sounds like the business is not evidence — as section "There are no replacements" below shows, the phrases that sound most like Husband Retail are the ones with no demand behind them at all.

The ten findings that decide the build

  1. The firm is effectively invisible in search. Twelve rankings in Semrush's entire worldwide database, every one of them on a project case-study page, and an estimated zero visits a month from all of them.
  2. English demand in Hong Kong is small and thin. Thirty-three terms carry genuine buying intent. Nobody wins this market on size; it is won by owning the whole set.
  3. Chinese is the real Hong Kong market, by a distance. On Google's own trend index, the leading Chinese shop-leasing words run several times ahead of every English equivalent, and three of the trade's most-used words have no English translation at all.
  4. Six of the eight Chinese pages have no English counterpart. They are not translations of the English plan. They are a second business.
  5. The incumbents are beatable on structure, not on size. The four big firms' Hong Kong retail pages run 224 to 1,309 words and carry zero current market figures between them. No FAQ, no question headings, no comparison table, no data.
  6. There is a market argument nobody on the page is making — sales up, vacancy at a post-pandemic low, and rents still falling. It resolves one way, and it is exactly Paul's argument.
  7. Husband Retail already ranks sixth on retail space consultant on a 184-word homepage with no H1 and the title tag "Home". That position is brand strength, not page work. The on-page job has not started.
  8. Job-board contamination is the single biggest trap in this vocabulary. It killed four terms outright and it sits across a third of the results page on several more.
  9. Two things are measured but unconfirmed: the Chinese demand has direction and no absolute figures, and 36 of the competitor-vocabulary terms come only from firms that rank on nothing in Hong Kong. Both are one session away from being settled.
  10. The build is not blocked on research. It is blocked on five decisions and two free accounts, listed at the foot of this tab.

3 · Chinese is the bigger market

The English list was measured first because that was the brief. The Chinese study of 24 August 2026 changes the picture, and it is the largest single finding in this tab.

How the words were found. Not by translating the English list — that was the point. 2,567 autocomplete phrases were harvested from what people in Hong Kong actually type, machine-filtered to 1,275, hand-picked down to 72, and every one of those 72 had its own Hong Kong results page read individually. 47 passed.

Three openings the Chinese reading exposes

  • The local market files its site by unit type, not by service. Centanet and Midland both organise around 地舖 / 街舖 / 商場 / 樓上舖 and repeat that quartet in every category title, because that is what the searcher has in mind. Our English plan organises around services. If the Chinese section copies the English structure, it will read as a foreign site.
  • 商舖空置率 — shop vacancy rate — is beatable today. It is held by Midland ICI's own report and the newspapers quoting it. Whoever publishes the number gets quoted, and the quotes carry links. That is one quarterly page, not a campaign.
  • The landlord side is under-served in Chinese. 租務管理, 租務管理公司 and 物業租務管理服務 are held by small local management firms — not by the big agencies. A credible adviser could take that page.

The rejects — including one that is a gap, not a dead end

Nine terms that look right and are not: 租盤 and 搵租盤 are residential (28Hse and House730 flat listings). 商場管理 returns Taiwanese content farms. 獨家代理 returns government pages about exclusive-agency law. 進駐香港 returns Immigration.

And four — 頂手, 頂手舖, 舖位頂讓, 商鋪轉讓 — return business-for-sale marketplaces and Facebook groups. The demand is real and not one property agency is present in it. Handing over a lease is a leasing question being answered by classified sites. That is a gap.

4 · What the competitors publish, and what none of them do

Two results pages were taken apart in full: commercial real estate hong kong on 24 August and retail space consultant on 26 August, every position read and quoted rather than scored.

The first thing the reading settled: which intent the page serves

commercial real estate hong kong is a listing search — CBRE, Centanet, Savills, Colliers, Knight Frank and 28hse property databases. Only two results on the whole page are not a database. A control term, retail leasing consultant hong kong, returns the opposite: Colliers Retail, Savills Retail Leasing, Knight Frank Retail Consultancy, CBRE Landlord Leasing — an advisory page.

Husband Retail is a consultancy with no property database and no intention of building one. So the page ranks on the listing term but converts on the advisory intent: it opens by naming the reader — owner, developer, investor — not by promising listings. That is a deliberate choice, written down before the build rather than discovered after it.

Seven things none of them do, six of which are parts of our own page blueprint:

  1. Not one current figure on four service pages between them.
  2. No question headings — statement headings throughout ("Making a difference").
  3. No FAQ, on the page or in the schema.
  4. No comparison table.
  5. No answer block — nothing an AI assistant can lift and quote.
  6. No named project evidence on the service page itself.
  7. No stated position on conflict of interest, though all four both advise landlords and broker deals.

The argument nobody on that page is making

Four facts from the Rating and Valuation Department and the Census and Statistics Department that look like they contradict each other:

  • Retail sales are up 9.6% in the first half of 2026.
  • Visitor numbers are up.
  • High-street vacancy is at a post-pandemic low.
  • And rents are still falling — down 3.4% on the year, 20.7% below the 2019 peak, and on Hong Kong Island 39.7% below the 2014 high.

They reconcile one way. The money is arriving, but it is not spreading. Jewellery and watches are up 20.1%; clothing 0.8%; footwear down 1.4%. Online is still only 9.4% of Hong Kong retail — the physical destination is not being replaced, it is being sorted.

Which means footfall is not the constraint. Tenant mix and positioning are. A centre can be full and still underperform, because rent per square metre depends on who is in it, not how many people walk past. That maps exactly onto the distinction Paul has drawn since Pacific Place — a destination brand versus a utility shopping centre — and onto the one thing he can say that the big four cannot: "we have worked with so many organisations and we saw good and bad practices … we can actually compare and others cannot compare."

No competitor on that results page makes this argument, because none of them puts a single current figure on the page.

The teardown of retail space consultant, 26 August

Thirteen positions read. Three of the top ten are job boards — the same contamination that killed leasing management and leasing agent. The rest split into two intents: who advises me on my retail property? (Savills, Knight Frank, SPACE, Husband Retail) and who plans the inside of my shop? (Studio C, Retail Rehab) or who fixes my retail business? (YCP, Clarkston, Bain). Husband Retail belongs to the first group and should write to it.

Three findings from that page:

  • Husband Retail already ranks #6 on a 184-word homepage with no H1, no services list, no contact call-to-action, and the title tag "Home". The one sales sentence the homepage owns sits in the meta description, where no visitor ever sees it. That position is brand and entity strength alone.
  • The two firms above it are Savills and Knight Frank — but what they are actually doing is a 400-word page and a reused template. This is not McKinsey-grade content. It is beatable on structure.
  • The closest structural peer, SPACE, ranks #10 on 145 words. Boutique peers are under-optimised for their own head terms.

Length is not the fight. The page at position 1 is 400 words. Depth of structure — audience named, services listed, proof named, people named, research attached — beats word count here.

The rejects were audited, not the picks

Exclusion errors are invisible by construction — nobody notices the term that was never on the list. So 190 rejected or parked terms were re-tested, and 47 were reinstated: 40 as applicable, 7 as adjacent. Sixteen of the 40 were the Gulf geography terms, killed in the first pass as "wrong market", which was factually wrong. Egypt was handled differently and correctly: reinstated only as adjacent — a real buyer in a market the firm has not evidenced.

Four reinstatements should not have passed — leasing1, research, regional research and expand in asia. are navigation fragments and broken slugs. They were left in the table rather than quietly deleted, because a silent deletion is exactly the invisible exclusion the audit exists to prevent.

The largest open question on this list

52 of the 66 surviving terms — and 36 of the 50 Hong Kong ones — are published only by firms that rank on none of the 40 Hong Kong terms we read. The single biggest source of this vocabulary is one Gulf consultancy.

Two readings, and this wave cannot settle which is right:

  • Whitespace. No Hong Kong firm has claimed the term, so it is uncontested and cheap.
  • Wrong vocabulary. Gulf, US and UK firms publish it because it suits their markets, and Hong Kong buyers simply do not use those words.

What is known: every one was read on a Hong Kong results page and came back commercial. What is not known is whether anyone in Hong Kong searches them at all. One authenticated Semrush session turns these 36 terms into either the best opportunity on the list or the fastest cut on it. Until then they are carried, flagged, and not built blind.

6 · What the earlier passes settled

Three earlier documents are superseded on detail but not on their lessons.

English demand for these services is worldwide, not Hong Kong (11 August). Four terms cleared the full gate — retail consulting and retail real estate as service pages, retail leasing on the condition that the page is unmistakably landlord-side, and retail development as an article and never as a service page. Seeding the word retail against the Hong Kong database returned brand names, jobs, Chinese-language queries and trade statistics: there is no Hong Kong consultancy search demand in English to capture. Two independent studies now point the same way.

Terms blocked on that pass, and why: retail consultant (job-seeker intent, two thirds of the page is careers listings) · retail property (transactional listings — wrong buyer) · luxury retail (shopper intent) · placemaking (definitional and academic) · retail consulting firms, retail strategy consulting, retail consulting services (the same query as retail consulting — separate pages would cannibalise the pillar) · tenant mix (the tool returned no rows at all; recorded as unmeasured, never as zero).

Long phrasings are dead ends (4 August). The whole first term list was built as "…consultant / consultancy / advisory + geography" and every one of those constructions came back empty or effectively empty, while the head terms behind them carry real demand. That pass overturned its own earlier ranking, which had ordered terms on the results page alone with no demand figure to weigh against it. It is why the house gate now requires volume, difficulty and a live intent read before anything is ranked.

Where the world market sits (12 August). Twenty-four service terms were added up per country. In demand order: United States (already researched, 20 terms confirmed, a 28-page tree) · United Kingdom · Australia · India · Canada · Ireland · UAE · New Zealand · South Africa · Singapore · Malaysia · Philippines · Hong Kong in English, last. The recommendation was Australia first, the United Kingdom second — Australia because retail still means retail property there and its terms are markedly easier; the UK because it is bigger, but it has to be sold as commercial property, since every UK phrase with "retail" in it belongs to recruiters or listing portals. India's fourth place is an artefact: a "retail broker" there is a stock broker.

And one decision that keeps recurring. Tenant representation is now a top opportunity in Hong Kong, the United States and Australia — while the dossier records that Husband Retail is not a tenant-rep broker. Three markets, one question, and it needs a single answer from Paul rather than three separate holds.
