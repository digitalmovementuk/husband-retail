Advice for the tenant, from people who have sat on the landlord’s side.

Husband Retail started on the landlord side in 1998, and many of our team worked for well-known landlords before they joined us. When we represent a brand, that experience is on your side of the table, from the first shortlist to the signed lease. The six sections below explain how the work runs and where it stops.

Landlord-side knowledge

Knowing how a landlord reads your offer

A landlord does not judge a tenant only on the rent offered. They look at how a brand fits the trade mix, what it does for footfall on that level and how it sits next to the tenants already signed. Our team has made those decisions for landlords, so we know which points carry weight in a negotiation.

Where a lease includes turnover rent, the tenant reports sales to the landlord every month. From the landlord's side, those reports show how each category trades per square foot. We have read those figures from that side, and it shapes how we look at the rent a location can carry for your brand.

An agent who has only worked deals sees the lease from one side. We use what we learned on the other side for the brand: which questions to ask before an offer, which terms to push on and where there may be room to move.

Choosing the location

The right centre for your brand

Hong Kong offers a brand many kinds of retail space, and they trade very differently. We work across mixed-use developments, standalone malls, premium outlets, casino and integrated resort retail and transit-oriented developments, so we can compare them on the same terms for you. Each type draws its own mix of shoppers at different times of the day and week.

For each option we look at who already trades there, who is coming, how people move through the building and which levels draw the strongest footfall. A good address on paper can still be the wrong unit if the people walking past it are not your customers. We also ask how often the tenant mix changes, because that affects how a unit trades over the length of a lease.

Because our team has planned and leased centres for landlords, we know how a centre is meant to work when it is finished. That helps when you are offered space in a mall that is still being built, and the plan is all there is to judge.

Store size and neighbours

Size and adjacencies, judged with care

On the landlord's side, store sizes and adjacencies are planned long before a brand is approached. The landlord decides which unit suits which trade and who sits next to whom. Having done that planning ourselves, we know how to judge a unit from the tenant's side.

For luxury brands this is a very sensitive matter. A boutique placed next to the wrong neighbour, or in a unit that is too large or too small for the collection, can weaken how the brand is seen in that location. We look at the unit, its frontage and the tenants on each side before we advise you to take it.

Brands outside luxury face the same questions about frontage, depth, floor level and neighbours. A lifestyle or sports brand needs the right unit as much as a fashion house does, and we give each brief the same attention, from the first shortlist to the final recommendation.

Negotiation

From heads of terms to the signed lease

Once the location is clear, we negotiate on your behalf. That starts with the heads of terms: the rent, the term, any turnover rent, rent-free periods, fit-out and the conditions that matter to your brand. We set out what you need and what you can trade before talks begin.

Our team has negotiated and concluded more than 500 tenancies. That experience covers many kinds of centre, and it helps us read where a landlord is firm and where a term is still open. A landlord's leasing team usually has aims for the trade mix as well as the rent, and knowing those aims helps us frame your offer in terms the landlord will take seriously.

We stay with the deal through the lease documentation until the lease is signed, and we check that the commercial points agreed in the heads of terms carry through to the final document. If a point changes late in the process, we explain what it means for your brand before you agree to it.

New to Hong Kong

Brands entering the market

Our first significant leasing mandate, in 2008, was to bring brands new to the market into a new centre. Representing brands came afterwards, and our tenant work has since included international fashion groups. That early work showed us what a brand needs to understand before it commits to a market it has never traded in.

A brand coming from overseas often knows its product and its customer well, but not how Hong Kong's centres compare or how a local lease is put together. We explain both in plain terms, then shortlist locations that fit your plans for the city.

We work as one team across borders. What opens in the US, Europe and the UK informs how we advise in Asia, so the comparisons we give you reflect what your brand is doing in other markets as well as here. Our projects span more than 20 markets across Asia, the Middle East and Europe.

Clear limits

What we do and where we stop

We act for one retail brand as a client at any one time, so you are never competing with another of our clients for the same space, and you know whose side we are on. We confirm this before we agree an appointment, and it holds for as long as we act for you.

Our tenant work covers market entry, up to the signed lease. We help you find the location, judge it and agree the terms. After signature, running the store is in your hands. If your plans later include further stores in Hong Kong or elsewhere in Asia, we can run the same process for each new location.

We do not consult on retail operations, and we are not designers. Store design, fit-out drawings and day-to-day trading stay with your own team or the specialists you appoint, and our advice stays on where to open and on what terms.
